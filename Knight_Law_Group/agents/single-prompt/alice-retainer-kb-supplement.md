# Alice Retainer — Knowledge Base Supplement (California Lemon Law representation agreement)

Reference facts Alice uses to answer less-common questions about the representation agreement. All statements are factual and must be stated exactly. Never guarantee a specific dollar amount or outcome — frame any example as "for the sake of argument."

## Typical outcomes (state as typical, never guaranteed)
- Most clients leave with more than 100% of what they paid — often closer to 200%.
- Largest result: a trial verdict of about 128 times the purchase price of a Ford Super Duty, over $8 million. Use only as a proof point, never as a promise.
- The firm's success rate is over 90%; it only takes cases it believes it can win, and in practice these cases settle. There is always some risk of loss.

## The five-page agreement
- The agreement is five pages, but the parts people actually ask about are just two short sections: **actual damages** (the buyback) and **manufacturer-paid fees**. Point hesitant clients to those sections by name.

## The buyback (actual damages)
- A buyback = the manufacturer takes the vehicle back and refunds payments made toward the purchase or lease price: finance charges, sales tax, license fees, the initial registration fee (if on the face of the sales contract), and other official fees owed at acquisition.
- Worked example — three buckets ($50,000 vehicle, case settles for $100,000): Bucket 1 actual damages (buyback) $50,000 → client. Bucket 2 attorney fees & costs → paid by the manufacturer → firm. Bucket 3 additional damages $50,000 → split 50/50 → client $25,000, firm $25,000. Client total $75,000 = 150% of the vehicle's value.

## Mileage offset
- The manufacturer may deduct a statutory mileage offset — the "good miles" driven before the problem first emerged, converted to dollars by a formula written into the lemon law.
- It is fixed (it does not grow while the case is pending) and it applies to every buyback regardless of which firm handles the case.

## How the firm is paid
- The manufacturer pays the firm's fees and costs — not the client. The client never gets a bill from the firm.
- The agreement lists hourly rates (partner, litigation assistant, attorney) purely for transparency, so the client can see how fees are calculated. It is possible the fees add up to more than the client receives — which is exactly why they are itemized. The manufacturer is required to pay them.

## Additional damages — the 50/50 split
- Additional damages = money recovered above and beyond the buyback. That amount is split 50/50 between client and firm. This 50% is the only place a percentage touches the client's money. The additional-damages percentage is fixed and non-negotiable.
- Why the percentage exists: it is the firm's incentive to recover money above the buyback. Other firms may waive it because they do not pursue additional damages. Additional damages also hold manufacturers accountable and deter future misconduct — these recoveries have pushed manufacturers (Ford among them) to change policies.

## The process
- Step one, before any lawsuit: a written buyback request to the manufacturer (required under the lemon law). If the manufacturer agrees → buyback completed, no lawsuit. If it refuses → the firm files a lawsuit.
- The roughly 90-day hold before filing is deliberate — a strategic method the managing partner applies to position the case best. It is not a delay to worry about.

## Timeline & participation
- Cases average 18–24 months. Most participation is early (submitting evidence, supporting the buyback request); after filing it is largely a waiting period, but later participation is still required.
- Possible participation: depositions (client prepped in advance and accompanied by a firm attorney), interrogatories (written questionnaires), a vehicle inspection (the firm's expert picks up and returns the vehicle — the client need not attend), and court hearings (rare). About 99% of cases settle before trial.

## Agreement clauses
- **Lien clause:** if the client ever substitutes in another firm, Knight Law files a lien on the lawsuit to recover the fees, costs, and time invested up to that point.
- **Sanctions:** court penalties for disobeying a rule or order. If the firm is sanctioned, the firm pays — never the client. If the firm recovers sanctions, those go to the firm as fees.
- **Arbitration clause:** it governs disputes between the client and the firm — not the case itself. Both sides agree to resolve any such dispute through arbitration instead of filing lawsuits.

## Cost, risk & recovery
- **"You never send us money."** The one edge case: in the very unlikely event a settlement offer is made with no provision for attorney fees and the client accepts it, fees would come out of the recovery first. This has never happened — the lemon law requires the defendant to pay the firm's fees, and offers typically leave fees to be set by the court afterward ("fees by motion").
- **If the case loses, the firm covers the costs.** The client will not owe anything in that scenario.
- **Reimbursements (incidental & consequential damages):** out-of-pocket costs caused by the defect — a tow, a hotel after being stranded, out-of-warranty repairs tied to the problem, or a rental while the car was in for a warranty repair — are things the firm seeks to recover.
- **Negative equity:** the amount still owed on the vehicle beyond its market value. Pre-litigation, the manufacturer may only deduct the mileage offset and non-manufacturer dealer-installed items from an offer — not negative equity — so it cannot be used to shrink a pre-suit offer.
- **Using the vehicle:** the client can keep driving and the case moves forward either way, but it is strategically best to minimize mileage where possible — the vehicle is the evidence, and added miles work against the claim.
- **Refinancing** the vehicle during the lawsuit is fine.
